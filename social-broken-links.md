### Variant 1 — Lead with the stat

📊 84%.

That's the share of working-age Canadians with disabilities who do not have Disability Tax Credit certification — and therefore cannot access the federal disability supports the DTC has come to gate, including the new Canada Disability Benefit.

In a new report for the Canadian Tax Observatory, my INCLUSIECON co-founder Dr. Gillian Petit dissects the certification process step by step and identifies four broken links in the pipeline:

→ Severity criteria that exclude episodic and mental-health disabilities
→ A 16-page form that overwhelms applicants without navigator support
→ The cost and inconsistency of medical practitioner sign-off
→ The absence of cross-program data sharing with CPP-D and provincial disability assistance

The fix doesn't require dismantling the DTC. It requires automatic certification for adjacent program enrolments, broader severity criteria, coverage of certification costs, and navigator capacity for low-income applicants.

If 84% of the population a benefit is meant to reach can't pass through its gate, the policy doesn't deliver on its promise.

### Variant 2 — Lead with the person

Big congratulations to my INCLUSIECON co-founder Dr. Gillian Petit on the publication of *Broken Links: Poverty and the Limits of the Disability Tax Credit* — her new report for the Canadian Tax Observatory.

The report tackles a structural problem hiding in plain sight. The Disability Tax Credit was designed in the 1980s as a tax-equity measure to recognize the higher daily costs of living with a disability. It now functions as the certification gate for at least 13 federal disability supports — including the Canada Disability Benefit, rolled out last summer.

The empirical finding: 84% of working-age Canadians with disabilities don't have DTC certification. Which means the majority cannot access the very supports the DTC has come to gate.

Gillian walks through the certification pipeline, identifies four specific points of failure — narrow severity criteria, the 16-page form, costly medical sign-off, and absent data sharing — and lays out concrete reforms that would let benefits actually flow.

This is precisely the kind of work INCLUSIECON exists to do: treating tax administration as benefit-delivery infrastructure, and showing that design choices made decades ago for one purpose now shape who can access programs designed for another.

### Variant 3 — Lead with the policy stakes

The Canada Disability Benefit was Ottawa's first serious attempt to reduce poverty among working-age Canadians with disabilities. To receive it, you need Disability Tax Credit certification.

84% of working-age Canadians with disabilities don't have it.

That's the central finding of *Broken Links: Poverty and the Limits of the Disability Tax Credit*, a new Canadian Tax Observatory report by my INCLUSIECON co-founder Dr. Gillian Petit.

The report walks through the certification pipeline — narrow severity criteria, a 16-page form, costly medical sign-off, and absent data sharing — and shows where each link breaks. The pattern of who gets filtered out is consistent: women, people with mental-health and episodic conditions, and the lowest-income filers. Exactly the groups for whom federal poverty rates are highest.

The fix is concrete: automatic certification for people already in CPP-D and provincial disability assistance, broader severity criteria, coverage of certification costs, and navigator capacity. Each was on the table when Ottawa tabled its Spring Economic Update in late April. None requires dismantling the DTC.

If 84% of the population a benefit is meant to reach can't pass through its gate, the policy doesn't deliver on its promise.
