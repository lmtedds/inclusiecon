Income and Social Supports: An Inclusive Systems-Based Framework for Cash Transfer Design

Lindsay M. Tedds and Gillian Petit
Canadian Public Administration · 2026 · doi:10.1111/capa.70050
ENABLE Cash Transfer Design Intersectionality Systems Thinking Disability Policy GBA+
Executive Summary

This paper develops a systems-based framework for the inclusive design and evaluation of cash transfer programs in Canada. Integrating policy design, empirical economics, and systems thinking, it moves beyond technocratic optimization toward "inclusion" — defined through an intersectional lens that accounts for overlapping systems of discrimination and disadvantage. The framework is structured into three interconnected stages: Structure, Components, and Evaluation. A retrospective analysis of the BC Basic Income Expert Panel serves as the case study.

01

The Inclusive Systems-Based Framework

01
Structure
The contextual backbone — identifying the population-at-risk, mapping structural environments and sites of power, and cataloguing the full system of existing income and social supports.
02
Components
The building blocks of program design — technical parameters (maximum benefit G, benefit reduction rate BRR, break-even level BE), operational features, and access pathways including administration and delivery.
03
Evaluation
Measuring impact at both individual and macro levels — ensuring the program enhances inclusion without leaving targeted groups worse off, assessed through an intersectional lens.
02

Defining Inclusion

The overarching goal of any income support program must be to enhance inclusion. The framework distinguishes two types, and insists both must be assessed through an intersectional lens — first articulated by Dr. Kimberlé Crenshaw — which examines how social categorizations (race, class, gender, disability) create interdependent systems of disadvantage.

Social Inclusiveness
Empowerment
Investing in human capital and enhancing opportunities for activities marginalized and vulnerable persons value, enabling fuller participation in community and economic life.
Relational Inclusiveness
Justice
Combating exclusion caused by resource deprivation — monetary, power, or human capital — and actively dismantling the systems and structural pathologies that perpetuate inequality.
03

Evaluative Criteria

Evaluation must be conducted through both individual and macro-level lenses. Prioritizing aggregate statistics while ignoring lived experience — or vice versa — produces an incomplete and potentially harmful assessment.

Individual Level — The Human Experience
Adequacy
Resources sufficient for community participation, measured against actual need — not merely statistical thresholds.
Accessibility
Identifying systemic barriers (language, literacy, technology) and the impact of stigmatization, which creates "othering" and reduces realized access.
Dignity
Evaluating the "welfare wall" and ensuring recipients are treated with dignity rather than paternalism. Nothing about us, without us.
Macro Level — Aggregate Impact
Economic Viability
Gross cost, financing methods, and fiscal impacts including effects on inflation or debt-to-GDP ratios.
Effectiveness
Changes in poverty rates, Gini coefficient, food insecurity, and "poverty efficiency" — persons removed from poverty per $1M spent.
Systemic Impacts
Effects on human capital, health care costs, crime prevention, and long-run public support for the program.
04

Case Study: BC Basic Income Expert Panel

The 2018 BC Basic Income Expert Panel applied a precursor to this framework to assess the feasibility of a basic income. It illustrates both the framework's utility and the gaps that arise when intersectionality and lived experience are not fully integrated.

Strengths
Detailed systemic mapping of interactions between existing provincial supports and proposed basic income models.
Clear objective-setting: basic income defined as a guarantee of minimum income that is simple, respectful, and inclusion-enhancing.
Simulation of thousands of parameter combinations (G, BRR, BE) to assess fiscal feasibility.
Identified Gaps
Intersectional papers were commissioned but did not feature prominently in the final report — ableism in the labour market, for example, was noted but not integrated.
Data constraints meant persons with disabilities could not be identified in tax-filer datasets, skewing results toward simpler "one-size" designs.
Qualitative lived experience was subordinated to statistical constructs like MBM thresholds, reducing transparency on how trade-offs were weighted.
